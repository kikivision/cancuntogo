Cancún sargassum season, month by month

Sargassum seaweed can turn up on Caribbean beaches in the warmer months. Here's when it hits Cancún, where it's worst, which beaches stay clearest, and how to plan around it.

2026 update · August

You are in the thick of it. August is the tail of the heaviest stretch of a heavy year — state authorities in Quintana Roo project roughly 119,000 tons reaching the coastline in 2026, against a previous record of about 96,000 tons in 2025, and the coast (Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Tulum) is still getting regular landings. As of late June the state's Sargassum Monitoring Network had 92.5% of beaches at its "excessive" level. The good news is what comes next: conditions typically ease through September and October, by late autumn the coast is usually back to clear, and there's more equipment aimed at the problem than in past seasons. If you're traveling now, the tips below matter more than usual — favor north-facing beaches and islands, book a resort with real daily cleanup rather than one that just mentions it, and check a live tracker within a few days of your trip. Conditions swing beach to beach on wind and current, so a report from last week tells you very little.

What sargassum actually is

Sargassum is a brown seaweed that drifts across the Atlantic in a vast belt and washes onto Caribbean shores, including Mexico's Quintana Roo coast. Out at sea it's harmless — even helpful — but when it piles up on the sand and decomposes, it browns the water's edge and gives off a sulfur smell.

The key thing to know: it's seasonal and highly variable. Amounts swing from year to year and even week to week with winds and currents, so a beach can be pristine one morning and have a band of weed the next. The patterns below are typical, not promises — which is exactly why timing and location matter so much. Planning around the calendar is also covered in our best time to visit Cancún guide.

Where it's worst — and where it isn't

Where it hits hardest

Sargassum rides in on easterly currents, so open east- and southeast-facing beaches catch the most. That means much of the Riviera Maya — Playa del Carmen, Akumal, Tulum — and the outer, ocean-facing stretches of the Cancún Hotel Zone are the most exposed in peak months.

Most exposed: open Caribbean beaches

Where the water stays clearest

North-facing and current-sheltered beaches fare far better. Isla Mujeres (especially Playa Norte), Isla Holbox on the Gulf side, Playa Mujeres, and the north-facing bay beaches around Punta Cancún tend to stay cleaner even when the open coast is hit. Cozumel's leeward west side is usually clear too.

Inside the Hotel Zone that line falls at roughly Km 9, where the strip pivots at Punta Cancún. North of it — Grand Fiesta Americana at Km 9.5, Breathless at Km 4.5 — the sand faces Bahía de Mujeres and stays usable on days the south strip is being raked. South of it, from Le Blanc at Km 10 down to Secrets Mirabel at Km 19.5, you're on the open Caribbean and exposure climbs the further south you go.

Clearest: north-facing beaches & islands

How to plan around it

How resorts manage it

Most beachfront resorts rake and haul sargassum off their sand every morning, and many run offshore booms or collection boats to keep their stretch clear. Effort and results vary a lot, so it's worth picking a resort that's known for staying on top of it. And pools, naturally, are never affected.

Tip: choose a resort with strong cleanup

What Mexico is doing about it

There's more machinery pointed at this than there used to be. In a federal announcement on July 30, 2026, President Claudia Sheinbaum set out a national anti-sargassum strategy worth more than MX$2 billion — reported as roughly US$115 million, though some outlets cite MX$2.634 billion and about US$150 million when counting spending through 2027. The first stage this year is MX$768.7 million for two tugboats, six coastal sargassum harvesting vessels, and around 50 km of new containment barriers, with Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Puerto Morelos, Tulum and Mahahual named as priority zones. At-sea collection capacity is set to rise from 1,227 tonnes a day to 1,870, with a target of 4,000 tonnes a day in 2027.

What that means for your trip: barriers and boats intercept sargassum before it lands, which reduces what reaches the sand — it doesn't switch it off. Plan your dates and your beach as if some will still get through.

Catching more at sea, but not all of it

Check before you go — and have a plan B

Close to your trip, check a sargassum tracker (the Cancún/Quintana Roo Sargassum Monitoring Network publishes color-coded beach maps, and satellite outlooks forecast the broader belt). Stay flexible where you can. And remember the rest of the trip is unaffected: cenotes, Isla Mujeres, Cozumel snorkeling, Chichén Itzá, and the eco-parks are all great even in a heavy week.

Common questions

What months is sargassum worst in Cancún?

Typically May through August, often peaking May to July. It usually starts arriving in March or April and clears by late October. Amounts vary a lot year to year, so no two seasons look exactly alike.

Which Cancún beaches have the least sargassum?

North-facing and current-sheltered beaches stay cleanest — Isla Mujeres (especially Playa Norte), Isla Holbox, Playa Mujeres, and the north-facing bay beaches around Punta Cancún. Open east- and southeast-facing beaches along the Riviera Maya and outer Hotel Zone catch the most.

When is there no sargassum in Cancún?

Roughly November through April is the cleanest stretch, with December to March the most reliable for clear water. That overlaps with Cancún's dry high season, so it's the safest window if a seaweed-free beach is a priority.

How do resorts deal with sargassum?

Most rake and remove it from their sand daily, and many use offshore booms or collection boats. Cleanup quality varies, so choose a resort known for staying on top of it. Pools are never affected.

Can you still enjoy Cancún during sargassum season?

Absolutely. Pools, cenotes, Isla Mujeres day trips, Cozumel's leeward side, and excursions like Chichén Itzá or the eco-parks are all unaffected. A sheltered beach or a resort with strong cleanup makes a big difference even in peak months.
